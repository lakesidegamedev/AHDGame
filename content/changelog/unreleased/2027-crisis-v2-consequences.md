## What changed

- Recessions, banking panics, debt crises, regional job shocks, energy shortages and waves of unrest now open by themselves when the conditions behind them hold for several turns in a row. The same kind of crisis cannot reopen in the same place for 24 turns after it ends.
- A crisis response now spends the holder's own actions for the turn instead of a separate currency, so responding competes with campaigning, legislating and running a business. Bigger responses cost up to three actions. A failed response gives the actions back.
- Blame follows exposure. A governor answers only for a regional crisis in their own state, a CEO only if the company operates in an affected sector and region, and party leaders and the legislature only for national crises.
- When a crisis moves into its aftermath, every office holder is judged on what they did. Acting against a serious crisis earns approval, and sitting on available responses while it was severe costs approval. Heads of state move national approval and governors move their state's approval.
- The same verdict moves the holder's party in elections where the crisis hit, by up to three percent of vote share, fading after 48 turns.
- Severe crises leave scars that heal slowly over time: lost regional output, damaged plant capacity, a loss of trust in institutions, people leaving a region, or a weaker credit standing.
- A crisis that stays severe long enough forces events: workers strike, depositors run on banks, lenders push a country toward default, the legislature calls for accountability, or a state of emergency is declared. A state of emergency unlocks stronger responses and slowly drains government approval while it lasts.
