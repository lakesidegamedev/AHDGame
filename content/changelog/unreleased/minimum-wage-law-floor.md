## What changed

- Behind a new switch, off until it is turned on, the national minimum wage law
  in force sets each country's wage floor as a share of the median wage. Until
  now these laws only moved income and poverty figures.
- Each option maps to a floor. Laws that name an hourly rate use that rate over
  a present-day median wage, so today's statutory options sit near real-world
  levels: about 28% of the median in the United States, 45% in Japan, 55% in
  Germany and 60% in Ireland. China's and Nigeria's options use fixed shares
  between 27% and 60%. Abolishing the minimum wage removes the floor.
- Industries that pay less than the floor raise their wages to it. With today's
  industry pay levels a floor at or below 50% of the median lifts nobody; above
  that, agriculture is lifted first, then retail, logistics and entertainment.
- Changing or repealing the law moves the floor from the next turn.
- The floor also feeds union pressure, as it already did: a weak floor adds a
  little to unionisation, a strong one takes a little away.
- When the switch is on, the law picker states the floor each option sets.
- A minimum wage set by an administrator still takes priority over the law.
