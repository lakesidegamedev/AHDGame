## What changed

- Two new laws open for filing at the start of the 2030 band: a retraining
  guarantee and a hiring subsidy. Two more open in the 2035 band: an automation
  pension levy and transition compacts with unions. All four come in five
  rungs, from no statute to a heavy version, for the US and the UK.
- Crew: automated plants no longer shed their full crew once the automation
  tax, the hiring subsidy or transition compacts are in force. The strongest
  version of each keeps a fifth to a quarter of the crew that would have gone,
  and no stack of laws keeps more than 45%. Fewer workers are displaced, and
  those plants pay more in wages.
- Costs: the automation tax and the pension levy raise the input bill of the
  four automation production methods, by up to 7% and 3% (10% together). The
  hiring subsidy cuts the wage bill of every firm in the country, by up to 5%.
- Jobs: retraining and the hiring subsidy speed up how fast displaced workers
  find new work. Wage insurance in the US and UK now does the same, which it
  did not before (only the other countries' version was read).
- Unions: transition compacts add up to 14 points to the union law bias that
  drives how many workers join unions.
- Pensions: the automation pension levy books a saving on the pension line of
  up to 0.28% of output at its top rung.
- Voters: enacting any of the six laws also moves the voter groups it helps or
  taxes (workers without a degree and low-wealth voters for the levies and
  subsidies, older voters for the pension levy, high-wealth voters pay), on
  top of the usual left and right swing. One enactment never moves a group by
  more than 6 points from these laws.
- Costs to the budget are small: the new laws cost between 0.005% and 0.45% of
  output at their top rungs, and the pension levy is a saving.
